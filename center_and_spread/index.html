Estimating the Center and Spread of a Distribution

Given a pile of numbers, “where is the middle?” and “how wide is it?” sound like they have one answer. They don’t: there are several estimators, and they disagree the moment the data are contaminated. This page runs four families of estimators on the same noisy sample and shows what each one sees.

The data

Most samples come from a Gaussian with mean \(\mu = 5\) and standard deviation \(\sigma = 1.5\). A fraction \(f\) are replaced by draws from a Cauchy distribution with the same center \(x_0 = 5\) and scale \(\gamma\):

$$x_i \sim (1-f)\,\mathcal N(5,\,1.5^2) \;+\; f\,\mathrm{Cauchy}(5,\,\gamma).$$

The Cauchy has no finite mean or variance: its tails are so heavy that a few samples land dozens of σ away. The goal for every estimator below is the same: recover center = 5 and spread = 1.5 of the Gaussian part, without being told which samples are contaminated.

1Mean and standard deviation

The textbook pair is the sample mean and sample standard deviation:

$$\bar x = \frac1N\sum_i x_i,\qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}.$$

They are the maximum-likelihood estimates for Gaussian data and, when the data are Gaussian, no estimator beats them. But both are built from sums of the raw values, and squares in the case of \(s\), so a single point that is far away has unbounded influence. A single sample at \(x = 5 + 100\) moves \(\bar x\) by \(100/N\) and adds \(100^2/N\) to the variance.

Mean ± standard deviation

Increase f a little. The mean drifts, and the standard deviation explodes: with the Cauchy contaminants it estimates the width of the outliers, not of the bulk. Because the Cauchy has infinite variance, \(s\) does not even converge as \(N\) grows. Press “New sample” a few times to see how erratic it is.

2Median and quantile-based estimators

Replace sums by ranks. The median is the 50% quantile; it only cares which side of it a point is on, so moving an outlier further out changes nothing. Spread can be read from quantiles too. For a Gaussian, the quartiles sit at \(\mu\pm0.6745\,\sigma\), so

$$\begin{aligned}\hat\sigma_{\rm IQR} &= \frac{Q_3-Q_1}{1.349},\\ \hat\sigma_{\rm MAD} &= 1.4826\;\mathrm{median}\,\lvert x_i-\mathrm{median}(x)\rvert,\\ \hat\sigma_{16\text{–}84} &= \frac{Q_{84.1}-Q_{15.9}}{2}.\end{aligned}$$

The constants rescale each quantity so that it equals \(\sigma\) for Gaussian data. Between the mean and the median lies the trimmed mean: discard the lowest and highest fraction \(\alpha\) of the sorted data and average the rest (\(\alpha=0\) is the mean, \(\alpha\to 0.5\) is the median).

The price: when the data are perfectly Gaussian (\(f=0\)) the median is about 25% noisier than the mean (its standard error is \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt N\)) and the MAD is noisier than \(s\). You pay a little efficiency for a lot of insurance. The box above the histogram is the usual box-plot: \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\).

3Sigma clipping

A compromise that keeps the familiar mean and \(s\): estimate, throw away points more than \(k\) standard deviations from the center, and repeat until nothing more is removed.

$$\text{keep } x_i \text{ if } \lvert x_i - c\rvert \le k\,\hat\sigma,\qquad c,\hat\sigma \text{ recomputed from the kept set each pass.}$$

The first pass is hamstrung because outliers inflate \(\hat\sigma\) (so the cut is too loose), but it removes the worst offenders, which shrinks \(\hat\sigma\) and tightens the next cut. Scrub through the passes below. The center \(c\) and scale \(\hat\sigma\) used for the cut can themselves be robust (median, MAD), in which case it converges almost immediately.

4Weighted means and standard deviations

Now suppose each measurement \(x_i\) comes with its own error bar \(\sigma_i\) (instruments differ, exposures differ). All samples measure the same true value 5, but the typical error bar is around 1.5 and varies several-fold between samples. Precise points should count for more. Weight by inverse variance, \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\):

$$\mu_w = \frac{\sum_i w_i x_i}{\sum_i w_i},\qquad \sigma_{\mu_w} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i}},\qquad s_w^2 = \frac{\sum_i w_i (x_i-\mu_w)^2}{\sum_i w_i - \sum_i w_i^2/\sum_i w_i}.$$

\(\sigma_{\mu_w}\) is the formal uncertainty of the weighted mean, and \(s_w\) is the weighted scatter. If the error bars are honest and there is no extra scatter, \(s_w\) should match \(\sqrt{N/\sum w_i}\) and \(\chi^2_\nu=\sum w_i(x_i-\mu_w)^2/(N-1)\approx 1\). A \(\chi^2_\nu\) far above 1 tells you the error bars are lying, which is exactly what contamination does.

Inverse-variance weighted mean

Weights only help if the error bars are true. With honest bars the weighted mean is the best unbiased linear estimator, tighter than the plain mean, and the contaminants are automatically down-weighted. If a contaminated point still claims a small error bar (the default), it gets a large weight and wrecks the mean; \(\chi^2_\nu\) then flags it. Clipping in units of each point’s own error bar is the fix.

5Which one should I use?

Everything above is one random sample. To compare estimators fairly, repeat the experiment many times at each contamination level, using the settings for \(N\) and \(\gamma\) from the controls at the top. The curves show the typical absolute error of each estimate (lower is better).

  • Clean, Gaussian data: mean and std are best; everything else is slightly noisier.
  • A few outliers: the center is easy to protect (median, trimmed mean, clipping all work); the spread is where mean/std fails first and worst.
  • Heavier contamination (to 25% here): median/MAD and both clipping variants stay close to the truth while mean/std keeps getting worse. Raise γ or N at the top and re-run to see how the gaps change.
  • Known per-point errors: use them, but check \(\chi^2_\nu\) and clip against each point’s own error bar.
