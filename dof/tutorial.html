Introduction

Fit a line to three points and something quietly strange happens: the three numbers you measured are not three independent pieces of information once you have estimated a slope and an intercept. This page builds the picture that explains why, and what it does to the uncertainty of your fit.

We will look at one tiny problem in two ways:

  • The traditional view: three dots in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane and a line through them.
  • The model view: the three \(y\) values stacked into a single point in \(\mathbb R^3\). A line \(y = a + b\,x\) can only reach a flat 2-D plane inside that space. Least squares drops a perpendicular onto the plane, and the leftover (the residual) lives in the one remaining direction: \(n - p = 3 - 2 = 1\) degree of freedom.

Then we add noise. Noise in \(y\) is a cloud in \(\mathbb R^3\); fitting flattens it onto the plane; and the flattened cloud, read in the coordinates \((a, b)\), is the uncertainty of the parameters.

The Setup

We observe three pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) and assume the data were generated by a straight line plus independent Gaussian noise:

$$\begin{aligned} y_i &= a + b\,x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, 3,\\ \varepsilon_i &\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2). \end{aligned}$$

Stack the three equations into vectors. Each observation gets its own axis, so \(\mathbf y\) is a single point in \(\mathbb R^3\), with \(\mathbf 1 = (1,1,1)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf y &= a\,\mathbf 1 + b\,\mathbf x + \boldsymbol\varepsilon \\ &= X\theta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad X = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf 1 & \mathbf x \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ 1 & x_3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

We do not know \(a\) and \(b\), so we estimate them by least squares: choose \(\hat\theta\) to make the leftover as short as possible.

$$\begin{aligned} \hat\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \hat a \\ \hat b \end{bmatrix} &= \arg\min_{\theta}\, \lVert \mathbf y - X\theta \rVert^2 = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y, \\[4pt] \hat{\mathbf y} &= X\hat\theta, \qquad \mathbf r = \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}, \qquad \mathrm{RSS} = \lVert \mathbf r \rVert^2. \end{aligned}$$

Notice what the residuals do as you move a point: they are never free. The residuals always sum to zero, and they are always uncorrelated with \(x\). Two constraints on three residuals. That is the first hint that only one of them is "free".

The Model View

Now forget the x-axis. Give each observation its own axis, so the whole dataset is one point \(\ycol\) in \(\mathbb R^3\). Every line \(a + b\,x\) predicts a vector $$\mathbf c = a\,\mathbf 1 + b\,\mathbf x.$$ As \(a\) and \(b\) vary, \(\mathbf c\) sweeps out a plane through the origin, spanned by \(\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf x\). The faint grid on the plane is the \((a, b)\) coordinate system.

Use the sliders to pick a model \(\ccol\). The distance from \(\mathbf c\) to \(\mathbf y\) is that model's total error. The closest point on the plane is \(\yhcol\), and the segment from \(\hat{\mathbf y}\) to \(\mathbf y\), the residual \(\rcol\), meets the plane at a right angle.

Why 1 degree of freedom? The plane is 2-dimensional (\(p = 2\)). Whatever is not in the plane must be perpendicular to it, and \(\mathbb R^3\) has only \(3 - 2 = 1\) perpendicular direction, the green \(\ucol\). So \(\mathbf r\) always points along \(\pm\mathbf u\), and the residuals carry exactly one number's worth of information.

The right angles are the normal equations:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r \cdot \mathbf 1 &= 0 &&\text{(residuals sum to zero)}\\ \mathbf r \cdot \mathbf x &= 0 &&\text{(uncorrelated with } x\text{)} \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X^\top \mathbf r = \mathbf 0 .$$

The effect of noise

So far the data were fixed. But y is a noisy measurement: had we run the experiment again we would have gotten a different y. Suppose the truth is the model \(\ccol\) and each dataset is $$\mathbf y = \mathbf c + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I_3).$$ In \(\mathbb R^3\) that is a round fuzzy cloud centered on \(\mathbf c\).

Fitting projects every point of that cloud onto the plane, which splits the noise into two perpendicular pieces. Writing \(P\) for the projection onto the plane and \(\mathbf u\) for the unit vector perpendicular to it:

$$\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \underbrace{P\boldsymbol\varepsilon}_{\text{in the plane (2 directions)}} + \underbrace{(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf u)\,\mathbf u}_{\text{perpendicular (1 direction)}} .$$

Each piece has its own consequence:

  • The perpendicular piece becomes the residual. It is a single coordinate along \(\mathbf u\), so \(\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\) is one squared standard normal: \(\chi^2_1\).
  • The in-plane piece moves the fit. Read in the \((a, b)\) coordinates of the plane, the flattened cloud is the sampling distribution of the parameters: \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat a, \hat b) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\).

The orange dataset is a single draw. To say how much the fit would wobble from draw to draw we need many datasets, and there are four standard ways to get them. They differ in where they are centered and where the noise comes from. Pick one below; both analyses that follow use it.

Cloud

Part 1: the perpendicular piece, and \(\chi^2\)

The residual space is one-dimensional, so the leftover noise has exactly one coordinate, \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf u \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\). Squared and divided by \(\sigma^2\), that is one standard normal squared, a \(\chi^2\) with 1 degree of freedom:

$$\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\lVert \mathbf r \rVert^2}{\sigma^2} = \left(\frac{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf u}{\sigma}\right)^{2} \sim \chi^2_1 .$$

This is the mechanism behind "df \(= n - p\)": each fitted parameter removes a direction that the leftover noise could have pointed along. The histogram below measures that squared length for 4000 datasets from the chosen cloud, against the \(\chi^2\) curve. Switch the quantity to see that the pieces add up: \(3 = 2 + 1\), the dimension of the plane plus the dimension left over.

Part 2: the in-plane piece, and parameter uncertainty

The other piece of the noise survives the projection. Because the parameters are a fixed linear map of the data, their covariance is the covariance of \(\mathbf y\) pushed through that map:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat\theta &= (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y, \\[2pt] \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta) &= (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \,\bigl(\sigma^2 I\bigr)\, X (X^\top X)^{-1} \\[2pt] &= \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}. \end{aligned}$$

For our design the matrix is small enough to write out, which shows where the tilt of the ellipse comes from:

$$X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & \sum x_i \\[2pt] \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat a, \hat b) = \frac{\sigma^2}{3\sum x_i^2 - \bigl(\sum x_i\bigr)^2} \begin{bmatrix} \sum x_i^2 & -\sum x_i \\[2pt] -\sum x_i & 3 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The off-diagonal term is nonzero whenever \(\sum x_i \neq 0\), that is whenever \(\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf x\) are not perpendicular: the \((a, b)\) grid on the plane is slanted, so \(\hat a\) and \(\hat b\) are correlated. Tick center x to replace \(\mathbf x\) by \(\mathbf x - \bar x\mathbf 1\), which is perpendicular to \(\mathbf 1\), and the covariance becomes diagonal:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(\hat a, \hat b) = \sigma^2 \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\lVert \mathbf x - \bar x\mathbf 1 \rVert^2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Covariance

What to take away. Truth and parametric clouds agree in shape, because \((\hat a, \hat b)\) is a fixed linear map of \(\mathbf y\) and only the center moves. In practice only the fit-centered ones are available, and each trades an assumption for accuracy: the parametric one assumes Gaussian noise and uses \(\hat\sigma\); the residual one assumes nothing about the shape but has just \(n - p = 1\) free residual direction to resample; the pairs one assumes nothing about the model but needs many more than three observations to stop producing degenerate resamples. The residual bootstrap also slightly understates the noise, because least squares makes residuals smaller than the true errors (a common fix rescales them by \(\sqrt{n/(n-p)}\)). Try shrinking \(\sigma\), spreading the \(x\) values, and switching modes to see which clouds respond.

Does your model fit?

Everything above asked how much a fit would wobble if the truth were known. Now turn it around: given one dataset, is the model a plausible explanation of it? If the line model is right and the noise is Gaussian with a known \(\sigma\), then the squared lengths of the pieces of \(\mathbf y\) have known distributions, and you can ask where yours landed:

$$\underbrace{\lVert \mathbf y - \mathbf c \rVert^2}_{\sigma^2\chi^2_3} \;=\; \underbrace{\lVert \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y} \rVert^2}_{\sigma^2\chi^2_1} \;+\; \underbrace{\lVert \hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf c \rVert^2}_{\sigma^2\chi^2_2}.$$

Why a steep line can still pass at large \(\sigma\). Every distance in these tests is measured in units of \(\sigma\). Raise \(\sigma\) and the same gap between the data and a line becomes a smaller number of noise-units, so more lines look plausible. In the picture, two things grow together:

  • The blue band widens: the uncertainty in \((a, b)\), which scales as \(\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\), now allows a larger family of lines. A line passes the \(\chi^2_2\) test exactly when its \((a, b)\) lies inside the 95% ellipse from Part 2, which is the same as the line staying inside the band over all \(x\): $$\frac{\lVert\hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf c\rVert^2}{\sigma^2} \le 5.99 \iff \bigl|c(x) - \hat y(x)\bigr| \le \sqrt{5.99}\,\sigma\sqrt{h(x)}\ \ \text{for all } x, \qquad h(x) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x\end{bmatrix}(X^\top X)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ x\end{bmatrix}.$$
  • The violet band widens: the \(\pm\sigma\) noise around your line now covers the data points, so the \(\chi^2_3\) error, \(\lVert\mathbf y - \mathbf c\rVert^2/\sigma^2\), is small.

The steep line passes because, at this \(\sigma\), the data cannot rule it out. That is a statement about how little three noisy points can tell you, not evidence that the line is right. Shrink \(\sigma\) and the bands tighten, and the same line fails.

What does “X% of noise draws are this large” mean?

Under each plot you will see a sentence like “If the model were true, 1.4% of repeated experiments would give a value at least this large.” It describes a thought experiment, and it is worth spelling out:

  1. Assume the model is exactly right: the truth is the line \(\mathbf c\) and the noise is Gaussian with the \(\sigma\) on the slider.
  2. Repeat the experiment over and over. Each repeat gives a new dataset \(\mathbf y = \mathbf c + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) and so a new value of the statistic (\(\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\), and so on).
  3. Ask what fraction of those repeats give a value at least as large as the one you actually measured. That fraction is the shaded area under the curve, the tail probability or p-value. The text under each plot also counts it directly in 4000 simulated repeats, so you can check the curve against the simulation.

So “1.4% of noise draws are this large” means: if nothing but noise of this size were going on, you would see a mismatch this big about once in 70 experiments.

What are you aiming for? Not for the biggest p, and not for the smallest error. You are aiming to not be surprised: the marker should sit where noise of this size ordinarily puts it, in the body of the curve and not out in either tail.

  • Why not maximize p? If the model really is right, p is spread evenly between 0 and 1, so 0.9 is just as likely as 0.1. A value of 0.99 is not extra good; it is as unusual as 0.01, and says the data are suspiciously close to the model (or that \(\sigma\) was set too large).
  • The cutoff is a convention. We call p below about 0.05 a right tail. But a perfectly correct model lands there about 1 time in 20, so one borderline value is weak evidence. Treat the tails as "worth a second look", not as a verdict.
  • "Good choice" means "no evidence against it." A typical value says the data are consistent with the model plus noise. It does not prove the model is right, and other models may be just as consistent.
  • Use the right reference curve. The \(\chi^2_3\) panel is only valid for an \((a, b)\) fixed before looking at \(\mathbf y\). If you chose \((a, b)\) by tuning it to these data, including "set to least squares", you have fitted two parameters, and the honest reference is \(\chi^2_1\), the best-fit panel. Comparing a tuned model to \(\chi^2_3\) is too lenient.
  • The \(\chi^2_2\) panel asks a different question: can the data tell your \((a, b)\) apart from the best line? A small tail means yes, your choice is detectably worse. A large p means the data cannot distinguish them, which is the same as your \((a, b)\) lying inside the ellipse from Part 2.

How to read it

  • Right tail (small p). The data are farther from the model than noise of this size usually puts them. Either the model is the wrong shape (here, the points bend and a line cannot follow), or the noise is larger than the \(\sigma\) you assumed.
  • Left tail (p near 1). The data are suspiciously close to the model, closer than noise of this size should allow. That usually means \(\sigma\) was overestimated, not that the fit is good. Note that for 1 df, small values are normal: \(\chi^2_1\) piles up near zero, so \(\mathrm{RSS} \approx 0\) is not suspicious at all.
  • Middle. The data are consistent with the model plus noise. That is evidence of no contradiction, not proof the model is right.

The catch: \(\sigma\) has to come from somewhere else. If you instead estimate $$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n - p}$$ from the same fit, then \(\mathrm{RSS}/\hat\sigma^2\) is identically 1 and the test says nothing. With 1 degree of freedom you cannot both estimate the noise and check the fit with the same three points. You need \(\sigma\) from outside the data (a measurement spec, a calibration, replicates), or more observations than parameters by a good margin.

Try this. The starting data lie close to a line, so the best line leaves a small \(\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\) and sits comfortably in the bulk of \(\chi^2_1\). Now click Load curved data (0, 3, 0): with \(\sigma = 1\) the best line has \(\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2 = 6\), out in the right tail (\(p \approx 0.014\)), because a line is a poor description of three points that bend. Raise \(\sigma\) to 2 and the same data are unremarkable, or drag \(y_2\) toward 1 so the points line up. Meanwhile your \((a, b)\) can be a bad choice even when the line family fits: the \(\chi^2_2\) panel shows how far it sits from the best line, which is the same question as whether the point \((a, b)\) falls inside the ellipse from Part 2 (the 95% contour is at 5.99).
