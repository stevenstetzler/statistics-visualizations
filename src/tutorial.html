Introduction

Fit a line to three points and something quietly strange happens: the three numbers you measured are not three independent pieces of information once you have estimated a slope and an intercept. This page builds the picture that explains why, and what it does to the uncertainty of your fit.

We will look at one tiny problem in two ways:

  • The traditional view: three dots in the x–y plane and a line through them.
  • The model view: the three y values stacked into a single point in ℝ³. A line y = a + b·x can only reach a flat 2-D plane inside that space. Least squares drops a perpendicular onto the plane, and the leftover (the residual) lives in the one remaining direction: n − p = 3 − 2 = 1 degree of freedom.

Then we add noise. Noise in y is a cloud in ℝ³; fitting flattens it onto the plane; and the flattened cloud, read in the coordinates (a, b), is the uncertainty of the parameters.

The Setup

We observe three pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ) and assume the data were generated by a straight line plus independent Gaussian noise:

yᵢ = a + b·xᵢ + εᵢ,   εᵢ ~ N(0, σ²),   i = 1, 2, 3

Stack the three equations into vectors. Each observation gets its own axis, so y is a single point in ℝ³:

y = a·1 + b·x + ε = X θ + ε,   X = [ 1  x ],   θ = (a, b)ᵀwith 1 = (1, 1, 1)

We do not know a and b, so we estimate them by least squares: choose θ̂ to make the leftover as short as possible.

θ̂ = (â, b̂) = argmin ‖y − Xθ‖² = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀ y
ŷ = Xθ̂,   r = y − ŷ,   RSS = ‖r‖²

Notice what the residuals do as you move a point: they are never free. The residuals always sum to zero, and they are always uncorrelated with x. Two constraints on three residuals. That is the first hint that only one of them is "free".

The Model View

Now forget the x-axis. Give each observation its own axis, so the whole dataset is one point y in ℝ³. Every line a + b·x predicts a vector c = a·1 + b·x. As a and b vary, c sweeps out a plane through the origin, spanned by 1 and x. The faint grid on the plane is the (a, b) coordinate system.

Use the sliders to pick a model c. The distance from c to y is that model's total error. The closest point on the plane is ŷ, and the segment from ŷ to y, the residual r, meets the plane at a right angle.

Why 1 degree of freedom? The plane is 2-dimensional (p = 2). Whatever is not in the plane must be perpendicular to it, and ℝ³ has only 3 − 2 = 1 perpendicular direction, the green u. So r always points along ±u, and the residuals carry exactly one number's worth of information.

The right angles are the normal equations: r·1 = 0 (residuals sum to zero) and r·x = 0 (uncorrelated with x).

The effect of noise

So far the data were fixed. But y is a noisy measurement: had we run the experiment again we would have gotten a different y. Suppose the truth is the model c and each dataset is y = c + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ²I). In ℝ³ that is a round fuzzy cloud centered on c.

Fitting projects every point of that cloud onto the plane. Two things follow:

  • The part of ε perpendicular to the plane becomes the residual. It is one standard normal (times σ), so RSS/σ² ~ χ²₁.
  • The part of ε inside the plane moves the fit. Writing the fit as coordinates (â, b̂) on the plane, the flattened cloud is the sampling distribution of the parameters:
(â, b̂)ᵀ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy  ⟹  Cov(â, b̂) = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ · σ²I · X(XᵀX)⁻¹ = σ² (XᵀX)⁻¹

In words: the covariance of the parameters is the covariance of y, pushed through the linear map that turns data into parameters. Because 1 and x are generally not perpendicular, the (a, b) grid on the plane is slanted, which tilts the ellipse: â and b̂ are correlated. Tick center x to make 1 ⟂ x − x̄ and the tilt disappears.

Where should the cloud come from?

Geometrically, the first three all keep the plane fixed and scatter y around a center, so fitting just projects the cloud onto the plane. The pairs bootstrap is different: resampling pairs changes x too, so every resample has its own plane and its own residual direction. There is no single plane to project onto, which is why the blue "fits" disappear in that mode.

Truth and noise

violet: possible datasets · blue: their fits on the plane

Each violet point is a possible dataset y = c + ε. Dropping it onto the plane gives the blue point, which is that dataset's fit. Only the in-plane part of the noise survives, which is why the blue points form a flat disc and why (â, b̂) lives in two dimensions.

What to take away. Truth and parametric clouds agree in shape, because (â, b̂) is a fixed linear map of y and only the center moves. In practice only the fit-centered ones are available, and each trades an assumption for accuracy: the parametric one assumes Gaussian noise and uses σ̂; the residual one assumes nothing about the shape but has just n − p = 1 free residual direction to resample; the pairs one assumes nothing about the model but needs many more than three observations to stop producing degenerate resamples. The residual bootstrap also slightly understates the noise, because least squares makes residuals smaller than the true errors (a common fix rescales them by √(n/(n − p))). Try shrinking σ, spreading the x values, and switching modes to see which clouds respond.
