Which variance-stabilizing transformation does my data call for?

Give this page a list of (x, y) pairs, or let it make some. It walks through the checks, one step at a time, and ends with advice on how to treat the scatter in your data.

The idea in plain words.

Some data have a problem: the bigger the values, the noisier they are. Think of weighing things: a feather is off by a hair, a truck is off by a lot. Many statistics tools assume the noise is the same size everywhere. If it is not, we can often fix that by re-scaling y with a function such as the square root or the log. That is a variance-stabilizing transformation. The steps below figure out which one, if any, your data need.

    1

    Plot the data

    Always start by looking. Draw x against y and fit the simplest line (or curve) through the cloud. The line is our guess for the average y at each x.

    Dots are your data. The dark line is the fitted average.

    2

    Look at what the line leaves over

    The residual (leftover) of a dot is how far it sits above or below the line. If the noise is the same everywhere, plotting leftovers against the line's height gives a flat horizontal band. A fan or funnel, narrow on the left and wide on the right, means the noise grows with the level.

    Each dot: its leftover (up/down) against the height of the line at that dot (left/right).

    3

    Sort the dots into bins and measure the noise in each

    You cannot see how spread out data are from one dot. So we line the dots up from lowest to highest line-height and cut them into bins with the same number of dots in each. In every bin we measure the typical size of the leftovers (the root-mean-square, or "spread"). If you took repeated measurements at the same x values, those repeats could be your bins instead.

    Dashed lines separate the bins. Each box shows ± one spread around zero.

    4

    Does the spread grow with the level? Draw the spread–level plot

    One dot per bin: the bin's average level against its spread, both on log scales. On log scales, "spread is proportional to level raised to a power b" becomes a straight line whose steepness is b. We fit that line, and also try five specific rules to see which one the dots follow best.

    constant → none SD ∝ √level → √y SD ∝ level → log y SD ∝ level² → 1/y proportions → arcsin √p

    Blue dots are the bins. The shaded band is the 95% range for the fitted slope. The thick colored line is the best rule.

    5

    Cross-check with the Box–Cox test

    λ (lambda) is a dial for re-scaling y. Raise every y to the power λ: λ = 1 leaves y alone, λ = ½ is the square root, λ = 0 means the log, λ = −1 is 1/y. Box–Cox tries every setting of the dial and gives each one a score for "does the noise look equal-sized and bell-shaped after this re-scaling?" It does this without bins, so it is an independent second opinion.

    Score against λ. Higher is better. The peak is the best λ. Settings above the dashed line cannot be ruled out at 95%. The black diamond is the answer from step 4.

    6

    Apply the transformation and check that it worked

    Re-scale y, fit the line again, and repeat steps 2 and 3 on the new leftovers. If the transformation did its job, the band is flat and the bins have the same spread. A good habit is to prefer an easy value like 0, ½ or 1 when it is inside the Box–Cox range.

    Conclusion: how to treat the scatter in your data

    The math behind the fits: E[y | x] and Var[y | x]

    When is this useful?

    Use it to…

    • Choose the scale before OLS, ANOVA or a t-test. They assume equal noise on the scale you analyze. A funnel in the residual plot is the cue.
    • Pick a model family. Slope b ≈ ½ points to Poisson-like noise, b ≈ 1 to a gamma or lognormal model, proportions to a binomial one. You may then fit a GLM instead of transforming.
    • Make plots and distances fair. Heatmaps, PCA and clustering of counts (RNA-seq, photon counts, image pixels) let high-level rows dominate until the noise is stabilized. Astronomers use magnitudes (log flux) for the same reason.
    • Compare groups on one yardstick. After stabilizing, one pooled SD describes every group, so intervals and power calculations are consistent.
    • Choose weights. On the original scale, weighted least squares with weights ∝ 1/level2b uses the same b.

    Be careful when…

    • The averages barely differ. Without a wide range of levels, every rule fits equally well. Step 0 will warn you.
    • There is a noise floor plus percent error. The spread–level dots then bend: flat at low levels and rising at high levels. No single power fits; look at asinh or a generalized log.
    • Counts are small or have many zeros. A Poisson or negative-binomial GLM is usually better than transforming.
    • You need the average on the original scale. Back-transforming gives medians, not means, unless corrected.
    • The line is the wrong shape. A curved relationship fitted with a straight line leaves leftovers that look like noise. Fix the average first.
    • Noise depends on something other than the level, such as time or batch. Transforming does not fix that.
