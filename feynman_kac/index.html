Feynman–Kac by Monte Carlo

The Feynman–Kac formula says a PDE's solution equals the expected value of a cost racked up by a randomly-wandering particle. That's an invitation to solve the PDE by simulation instead of algebra: throw a swarm of random particles at it and average. This page does exactly that, live in the browser, and shows how cheap it is.

1The setup

Take the worked example from the Wikipedia article: the convection–diffusion equation

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},\qquad u(x,1) = e^{-x^2}.$$

has the closed-form solution \(u(x,t) = (5-4t)^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-(1-t+x)^2/(5-4t)\big)\). Feynman–Kac says the same \(u(x,t)\) is also

$$u(x,t) = \mathbb E\big[e^{-X_T^2} \,\big|\, X_t = x\big],\qquad dX_t = dt + \sqrt 2\,dW_t,\quad T=1,$$

i.e.: start a particle at \((x,t)\), let it jitter forward under this specific drift-plus-noise rule until time \(T=1\), score it by \(e^{-X_T^2}\) wherever it lands, and average that score over many independent particles. That average is \(u(x,t)\) — no PDE solver required.

2The particle swarm

Each thin line below is one simulated path \(X_s\) for \(s\in[t_0,1]\), stepped forward with the Euler–Maruyama method: \(X_{s+\Delta t} = X_s + \mu\,\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\) for a fresh standard-normal draw \(Z\) at every step. The drift \(\mu=1\) pushes every path to the right; the noise \(\sigma=\sqrt2\) fans them out.

4Averaging converges — and it's cheap

The Feynman–Kac estimate is just the running average of the payoff over paths simulated so far. By the central limit theorem its standard error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt N\), so the band below narrows as more paths are thrown in, converging on the dashed exact value. The timing line underneath is the actual wall-clock cost of the whole simulation on this page, in your browser, right now.

Monte Carlo estimate of u(x₀, t₀) vs. number of paths

Drag N up to its max (20,000 paths × up to ~450 steps each) and watch the timing line. On ordinary hardware this stays well under a tenth of a second — the simulation is a few hundred thousand multiply-adds, and every path is independent, so there's nothing to parallelize or optimize beyond what a single for loop already does. The expensive regime for Monte Carlo methods is high-dimensional state or extremely fine time steps, neither of which this toy problem needs.

5Why this works

This isn't a coincidence or an approximation trick bolted onto the PDE — it's the content of the theorem. Feynman–Kac proves that any solution of a PDE of this form equals an expectation over paths of the matching stochastic differential equation. So simulating particles isn't a heuristic stand-in for solving the PDE: it's a different, valid way of computing the exact same number, at a cost that scales with how precisely you want the answer rather than with how hard the PDE is to discretize. That's why Monte Carlo methods are the tool of choice for Feynman–Kac problems in high dimensions — pricing options on baskets of dozens of correlated stocks, say — where a grid-based PDE solver would need a grid with astronomically many cells, but simulating one more random path costs exactly as much as it always did.
