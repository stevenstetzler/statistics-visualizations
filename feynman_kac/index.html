The Feynman–Kac Formula

The Feynman–Kac formula says that the solution to a certain kind of partial differential equation, at a point \((x,t)\), equals the expected value of a "cost" racked up along a randomly-wandering path that starts at \(x\). It's a bridge between two very different-looking worlds: deterministic PDEs solved on a grid, and random particles simulated path by path. This page builds that bridge piece by piece, with a live simulation at every step.

1The one-sentence idea

Pick a point \((x,t)\). On one side is a PDE's solution \(u(x,t)\) — a single deterministic number. On the other side is a swarm of random particles, all starting at \(x\) and wandering forward in time. Feynman–Kac's claim: \(u(x,t)\) is exactly the average outcome of that swarm. 2Setting up the pieces

Three ingredients go into the formula. Each gets its own small widget before they combine.

2a. The random path

The particle is an Itô diffusion: \(dX_s = \mu\,ds + \sigma\,dW_s\). The drift \(\mu\) pushes it in a direction; the noise \(\sigma\,dW_s\) (Brownian motion) jitters it. Drag both and watch the swarm fan out, and where it ends up at \(s=1\).

2b. Running cost and discounting

The particle can also incur cost as it moves, at rate \(f(X_s,s)\), while "decaying" at rate \(V(X_s,s)\) — so cost incurred far in the future counts for less, via the discount factor \(e^{-\int_t^s V\,dr}\). Drag the time slider to scrub along one fixed path and watch both meters move: the cost bar fills as the particle accumulates \(f\), the discount bar drains as decay compounds.

Here \(f(x,s)=|x|\) (cost grows with distance from 0) and \(V(x,s)=1.2\) (constant decay rate), just to illustrate the shapes — not tied to the worked example in §5, which has no cost or decay term.

2c. Terminal payoff

At the fixed horizon \(T\), the particle incurs one final lump cost \(\psi(X_T)\), wherever it happens to have landed. The histogram in §2a already shows the first half of this — where particles end up; §5 below adds the payoff function on top of exactly that kind of histogram.

3The theorem, stated

For the PDE

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mu(x,t)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \tfrac12\sigma^2(x,t)\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - V(x,t)\,u + f(x,t) = 0,\qquad u(x,T)=\psi(x),$$

the solution is the expectation

$$u(x,t) = \mathbb E\left[\,e^{-\int_t^T V(X_s,s)\,ds}\,\psi(X_T) \;+\; \int_t^T e^{-\int_t^\tau V(X_s,s)\,ds} f(X_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau \;\middle|\; X_t = x\,\right]$$

where \(X_t\) is exactly the particle from §2: \(dX_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t\). Matching each piece to the story above:

u(x,t) = E[ discount · terminal payoff ψ(X_T) + ∫ discount · running cost f dτ  |  starts at x, time t ]
discount e^(-∫V ds) ↔ the decay meter in §2b running cost f ↔ the cost meter in §2b terminal payoff ψ(X_T) ↔ the histogram overlay in §2c / §5 conditioning X_t=x ↔ the launch point in §1 / §2a

Read as "expected cost-to-go": a particle starting at \((x,t)\), accruing running cost, decaying so future cost is discounted, with one final cost at the end — \(u(x,t)\) is the expected total.

4Why it's true (sketch, not full proof)

Define the discount process \(g(t,s)=e^{-\int_t^s V(X_r,r)\,dr}\) and \(Y_s = g(t,s)\,u(X_s,s) + \int_t^s g(t,\tau) f(X_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(u(X_s,s)\) and differentiating \(Y_s\), the PDE from §3 turns out to be exactly the condition that makes the drift term of \(dY_s\) vanish — i.e. exactly what's needed for \(Y_s\) to be a martingale (a process whose expected future value, given the present, is its current value). Once that holds, \(u(x,t)=\mathbb E[Y_t]=\mathbb E[Y_T\mid X_t=x]\), and \(Y_T\) is exactly the bracketed expression in §3. The full algebra is in the Wikipedia derivation; here's the martingale property itself, made visible instead.

5Worked numerical example

The convection–diffusion equation \(\partial_t u + \partial_x u = -\partial_x^2 u\) with \(u(x,1)=e^{-x^2}\) has the closed form \(u(x,t)=(5-4t)^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-(1-t+x)^2/(5-4t)\big)\), and equivalently \(u(x,t)=\mathbb E[e^{-X_T^2}\mid X_t=x]\) with \(dX_t = dt + \sqrt2\,dW_t\), \(T=1\) — no cost or decay terms, just drift, noise, and a terminal payoff. This is the example worked out in the Wikipedia article; everything below runs it live.

6Generalization: when the particle runs until it hits a wall

So far time runs for a fixed horizon \(T\). The formula generalizes to elliptic PDEs too, with no time variable at all: instead of stopping at \(T\), the particle runs until it first exits a bounded domain \(D\), at a random exit time \(\tau_D\). The boundary condition \(\psi\) is then paid wherever it exits (the Dirichlet problem). Take the simplest case: pure Brownian motion (no drift) on the interval \((a,b)=(-2,2)\), with \(\psi(a)=0\), \(\psi(b)=1\). With no drift the PDE is just \(\tfrac12 u''=0\), so \(u\) is linear: \(u(x) = (x-a)/(b-a)\) — the same straight line you'd get by linear interpolation.

This is a genuinely different picture from §1–5: there "wander for a fixed time," here "wander until you hit a wall." The same formula covers both, and the N-dimensional case covers a particle diffusing in a domain of any shape in any number of dimensions — just harder to draw.

7Applications

Finance. In the Black–Scholes model, a stock price \(S_t\) follows geometric Brownian motion, \(dS_t = (r\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t)S_t\). A European call option pays \((S_T-K)^+\) at expiry \(T\), and the risk-free rate \(r\) plays the role of the decay rate \(V\). Plugging this into Feynman–Kac recovers the Black–Scholes PDE exactly — so the no-arbitrage option price is a Feynman–Kac expectation, and can be computed by simulating stock-price paths and averaging the discounted payoff, exactly like §5.

Quantum mechanics. The same machinery, run in imaginary time, underlies diffusion Monte Carlo methods for solving the Schrödinger equation in quantum chemistry — a genuinely different story (it needs the complex-valued case Kac's proof doesn't cover), so just a pointer here rather than a widget.

8Where it came from

Richard Feynman and Mark Kac were colleagues at Cornell in 1947. Kac attended a talk of Feynman's and realized that Feynman's path-integral heuristic for quantum mechanics — real-valued paths weighted by a complex phase — had a rigorous probabilistic counterpart once the weight was made real-valued, via exactly the kind of expectation in §3. Kac published the result in 1949, originally as a way to compute distributions of certain Wiener functionals. The complex-valued case actually needed for quantum mechanics is, per the Wikipedia article, still an open question.
